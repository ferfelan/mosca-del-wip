WIP limit workshop: a 60-minute session with a game

It is called “Stop starting, start finishing” (in Spanish, “Deja de empezar, empieza a terminar”). The team counts how many things it has in progress, watches the fly work one day with 9 tasks at once and another with 2, and leaves with its own WIP limit written down, what it does when something is left waiting, and a two-week test with a date.

By Felipe FernándezUpdated on {{fecha}}Before the first pilot

The workshop material is in Spanish for now. The slides, the facilitator’s quick guide and the team sheet are written in Spanish. The game and the report are available in English, and the WIP limit guide explains the ideas the workshop uses. If you facilitate it in another language and want to share how it went, write to Felipe on LinkedIn.

What the workshop is

A 60-minute session for a team to find its work-in-progress limit using the game Mosca del WIP live. It is not a talk: the team counts its real work, watches what happens to the fly and decides on a number to try.

The session opens with a question, how many things do you have in progress today, and continues with two days of the game projected at normal speed: one with 9 tasks at once, another with 2. Each day lasts a minute. In the game, with 2 tasks the fly finishes 18 and with 9 she finishes 9, even though she is busy all day. The ideas that are still missing fit in five three-minute slides, and the rest of the time belongs to the team: filling in its sheet with its real work and agreeing on the test.

The facilitator doesn’t propose the number. What gets proposed is the path: a little less than what they have today, and if they argue between two, the lowest one everyone agrees to try.

What the team takes away

Three things written on its sheet: its starting limit for things in progress, what it will do when something is left waiting instead of starting something else, and a two-week test with the review date on the calendar.

  1. Today we have __ things in progress. Counted on the board, as is. If two people share one, it counts once.
  2. Our starting limit: __ A little less than today, uncomfortable but possible. The simulation is of a single fly and doesn’t give a number for teams.
  3. When something is left waiting, instead of starting something else, we will: __ Written as something you do, such as helping unblock what’s waiting, asking for help or finishing something already started.
  4. We try it for 2 weeks and review it on __ In a meeting you already have. Meanwhile, on your usual board: the limit written on the In progress column and, on each card, the day it started and the day it finished.

We don’t promise a result: it depends on each team and on what happens around it. What does leave the room is the team’s capacity in plain sight, an agreement of its own and a date to look at its data.

Sixty minutes in four parts

1Connection10 min 2Concepts15 min 3Practice25 min 4Closing10 min

  1. 1Connection 10 min

    You ask how many things they have in progress and play two days of the fly, one with 9 tasks at once and one with 2, at normal speed. Question for the room: which of the two days does your week look like?

  2. 2Concepts 15 min

    Five three-minute slides, each with its own question. Every number says where it comes from: in the game, in the simulation or according to studies. The question that helps most: who or what do you wait on the most?

  3. 3Practice 25 min

    The team fills in its sheet with its real work, line by line, counting on its board. Question for the room: when you reach the limit and your thing is waiting, what will you do instead of starting something else?

  4. 4Closing 10 min

    You explain the test, which lives on their usual board, someone reads the sheet out loud and each person says one sentence. Photo of the sheet and the review on the calendar before leaving.

The four parts follow Sharon L. Bowman’s structure in Training from the BACK of the Room! (Pfeiffer, 2009).

The downloads (in Spanish)

Four free files: the editable slides and a PDF of them, the facilitator’s quick guide and the team sheet to print. The slides use the fonts Lora and Figtree from Google Fonts; Google Slides has them, and PowerPoint or Keynote need them installed.
